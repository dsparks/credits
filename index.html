Team-Building Credits Proposal Simulator

Team-Building Credits

Reforming the NBA draft lottery by realigning incentives and allocating talent to teams that need it.
Drew Cannon · David Sparks · Mike Zarren
Boston Celtics

Tanking is just a rational response to the existing incentives.

Let's change those incentives!

Team-Building Credits sever the connection between losing and draft position, giving teams direct control over their rebuilding strategy.

The league distributes ≈100 Credits to each team, each year. Fewer Credits go to teams who advance in the playoffs.

Credits are used to bid for draft position in a Rolling Window Lottery.

But unspent Credits carry over year-to-year, allowing teams to save up.

Want a top pick? Save up your Credits or trade for them, then bid when the timing is right.

Credits can be traded, just like players, picks, and cash are now.

Future Credit allocations can also be traded, as can pick slots

Fans deserve a league where their team never has a reason to lose.

The Problem

Goodhart’s Law and the Draft

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

For competitive balance reasons, and for fans to have hope, it is desirable to give teams in need of talent an advantage in acquiring good young prospects. Unfortunately, the way the league has historically identified teams in “need” of talent is to look at regular season losses, and this measure of need has become a target which teams can strategically pursue, to the detriment of the basketball product.

Ideally, we could find some measure of need that is not directly manipulable by teams. But nearly all proposed lottery reforms either fail to disentangle “need” from losing, or they fail to provide adequate relief for teams with true need. Counting wins after a certain point in the season creates an incentive for good teams to lose early, and doesn’t help teams that simply cannot win at any point. Restricting pick protections to narrow ranges just causes teams to pool toward those breakpoints. Considering record across multiple seasons creates an incentive to keep losing once you’ve started. Disallowing consecutive top-four picks makes it harder for talent to reach the teams that need it most.

Each of these proposals merely shifts the timing or breakpoint at which losing becomes the rational long-term strategy.

The Solution

Team-Building Credits

The Team-Building Credits system gives teams direct control over their relative advantage in acquiring talent, letting them determine need for themselves, and removing the incentive to falsely signal need by intentional losing. Credits cannot be manipulated through non-competitive strategies or lineups.

How It Works

Each year, the NBA allocates 100 Team-Building Credits to each team, minus deductions for playoff success. Credits may be saved or traded, but their primary use is to bid for draft position.

On a date in early May, teams simultaneously submit sealed bids to the league. Bids must be integer amounts, and teams must have enough Credits to cover the bid. After the deadline, the league reveals all bids publicly.

Picks are allocated through a Rolling Window Lottery: the top four highest-bidding teams enter a lottery for the first pick, weighted by bid size. The winner is removed, the next-highest bidder enters, and the process repeats for each subsequent pick. The winner of each window is charged an amount equal to halfway between their own bid and the lowest bid in that window — so a team that bids high but falls to a later pick retains Credits for other uses.

Simulate the full lottery process

Teams that bid zero are assigned picks at the end of the first round, with a random tiebreaker.

Second-round picks are allocated through a second stage of bids, which happens after the Rolling Window Lottery, in order of bid size.

Talent allocation

The NBA has historically prioritized giving non-contending teams the opportunity to build through the draft. Team-Building Credits preserve that opportunity. Teams can save Credits for the years in which they most acutely need draft talent, then invest heavily to maximize their chances. A rebuilding team can also trade veteran talent for additional Credits, further improving their draft position.

Trade market

Credits can be used as consideration in any trade, just like player contracts, portions of draft picks, and cash. A single Credit may serve as minimum consideration, replacing the classic heavily-protected second-round pick. Teams can also trade first- or second-round draft slots, or full future Credit allocations (all subject to Stepien-like rules), though these carry no protections or swap options, because the Credit bidding process makes those mechanisms irrelevant.

Teams holding multiple slots in a given year bid separately for each. Between the lottery and draft night, teams can trade specific picks to move up or back, as they do today.

Shaping Incentives

Offsets and Enforcement

Credit offsets for playoff success

The system need not allocate the same number of Credits to every team. Our proposal deducts 20 Credits for each playoff series won in the immediately preceding season. The NBA champion starts with 20 Credits; a team eliminated in the conference semifinals receives 80. This reflects the cyclical pattern of team success and gives teams outside contention better opportunities to improve, without creating an incentive to lose regular season games.

Credit penalties for rules enforcement

Because the league is the sole source of Credits, it can use this power for rules enforcement. Where monetary fines are currently used — player participation, injury reporting, tampering — replacing those fines with Credit deductions makes the penalties costly in basketball terms rather than financial ones. Docking a team a few future Credits is more granular and less heavy-handed than taking away future draft picks, which is one of the few recourses currently available.

But Credits could be used in support of any league goals. For example, the league could award Credits to In-Season Tournament finalist teams, or to the teams of the players on the winning All-Star team.

Implementation

The Transition Period

The first Credit-bid draft would realistically take place in 2034 at the soonest. Once agreed to, all existing pick trades are honored, and teams continue trading picks under current rules through the 2033 draft.

In the first year after agreement, teams can trade 2034 picks and beyond as whole slots under the new rules. Teams can also trade their full allocation of Credits for a specific future year, up to seven years out.

Starting July 1, 2034, the league distributes Credits to all teams. To smooth the transition to the new system, we recommend the league begins distributing smaller allocations of Credits to teams roughly three years before full implementation. These Credits could be traded immediately, or eventually used to bid on draft position.

A possible transition ramp could look like this:

Year Credits to each team Playoff series offset
2031 25 -5
2032 50 -10
2033 75 -15
2034 (full implementation) 100 -20

This would allow for an average of 225 Credits per team as the system begins. And of course these would be tradeable as soon as they’re allocated to teams.

Questions & Answers

Q & A

What does the annual schedule look like?
  • July 1: The league allocates 100 Credits per team, minus playoff offsets.
  • Late April: Early entry deadline for the NBA draft — same timing as current system.
  • Early May (after the start of the conference semifinals): Teams submit their Credit bids for the Rolling Window Lottery.
  • Mid May: The Rolling Window Lottery is held — same timing as current lottery.
  • Late May: Teams submit Credit bids for second round picks, and the second round order is revealed soon thereafter.
  • Late June: The NBA draft.
Why the Rolling-Window Lottery?

The Rolling-Window Lottery gives teams significant control over where they pick, as pick allocations are largely dictated by bid size. However, a small infusion of lottery luck ensures that teams can’t perfectly aim for a particular spot in the draft. At the same time, the Vickrey-style debiting ensures that high bidders get substantial Credit savings if they slip in the draft.

We prefer this model to several others we considered:

  • Deterministic bid order — picks allocated in order of bid, with random tiebreakers. Almost no randomness; high bidders can’t fall, low bidders can’t rise.
  • Full lottery — each pick is drawn from a lottery over all bidders, weighted by bid. Highest randomness; greatest potential for high bidders to fall and low bidders to jump.
  • Top-N lottery — top several picks awarded by lottery, all other picks allocated in order of bid. Moderate randomness; high bidders unlikely to fall far, some small probability low bidders jump.
How will the draft-pick penalty for teams in the Second Apron be implemented?

The first step of the penalty is easily carried over: we can simply “freeze” that team’s first round draft slot in the seventh following season, preventing them from trading it.

If a team is in the Second Apron for at least 2 of the next 4 cap-years, that team would then be prevented from bidding Credits toward that frozen pick, ensuring that it lands at the end of the first round.

This would be slightly less harsh than the current penalty since other teams could also bid 0 and thus this pick could be randomly drawn to be higher than 30 if there are other teams bidding zero. More importantly, the penalized team could still carry that year’s Credits over to a future year, so the penalty is less harsh than it is now, where all your “pick quality equity” for that year is taken away.

How will this system handle expansion?

It sounds like current expansion discussions will be implemented prior to this system, but regardless, the Team-Building Credits system handles expansion straightforwardly.

Unlike other systems that require fixed ranges of picks, the Rolling Window Lottery works for any number of picks – the lottery simply rolls through a few additional windows.

The only thing that would need to be determined in an expansion scenario after 2034 is how many Credits the team would be allocated initially. This is not really different than how the league determined, during previous expansions, how teams were eligible for top picks. And of course, as it effectively has before, the league could limit the picks for which an expansion team is eligible – it wouldn’t be hard to say “Expansion Team X can’t be in the top 3 lottery windows, regardless of how much they bid.”

Why 100 Credits? Why a four-team Rolling Window? Why 20 Credits per series win? Etc.

We chose these numbers after experimenting with other plausible values, but none of them are set in stone, or strictly necessary for the system to work.

100 Credits is a practical scale: it is simple, legible, and large enough to avoid making each single Credit overly important. A much smaller number would make bids too coarse; a much larger one would mostly amount to arbitrary inflation.

A four-team Rolling Window provides a good balance between agency and randomness. With fewer teams in the window, draft order becomes too deterministic; with more, the relationship between bid size and draft position becomes too weak. The simulator makes those tradeoffs easy to inspect.

Twenty fewer Credits per playoff series won is just one reasonable way to tilt more Credits toward less successful teams. That schedule could be made flatter, steeper, or nonlinear without altering the core structure. And even with no playoff-success offset, we would expect Credits and talent to flow through the market toward the teams that value them most.

We are open to evaluating other values for each of these things; our proposal is intended as a starting point.

How is this different from other credit/income-based proposals?

Several other proposed systems share a common intuition with ours: give teams a resource and let them spend it according to their own preferences and needs.

In contrast with those proposals which require the league to “correctly” set the prices for picks or rebates, or the size of lotteries into which teams can bid, our proposal lets the market decide all these things. Any price the league sets in advance will be wrong in some years; a market of 30 informed bidders reprices every season and should direct Credits and talent toward teams that need them.

Given that every team’s bankroll will be known by all the other teams, we’d expect the rolling window lottery to efficiently price the various picks (and rebates) better than a predetermined system could.

Why trade “pick slots” at all, in addition to Credits and future Credit allocations? Why not just let teams submit multiple distinct bids for more than just one pick in the first round?

It would be possible to implement a Credits-like system, but have teams “bid into” the first round – you could even use the Rolling Window Lottery. But there are several advantages to explicitly allocating pick slots to teams:

  1. Having future picks to trade is actually a feature:
    1. It maximizes continuity with the current system; teams are accustomed to being able to trade future picks.
    2. Having future picks available as an asset supports liquidity in the trade market.
  2. Bidding on an openly-known number of picks, as stipulated in our proposal, is already a non-trivial evaluation problem for teams. If teams could “bid into” the draft, it requires either a variable number of first round picks, or it necessitates that some teams will push others all the way out of the first round. Either of those mechanisms further complicates an already complicated planning process for teams.
  3. It’s also useful for team planning purposes to know what (future) assets they and other teams hold.
  4. Under our proposal, teams can still make multiple first round picks in the same draft, they just need to trade for other teams’ slots.
